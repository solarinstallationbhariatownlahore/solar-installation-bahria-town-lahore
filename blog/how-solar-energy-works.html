How Solar Energy Works

Sunlight becomes electricity in your home through panels, an inverter and your wiring. Here is the journey, step by step, in plain language.

From Sunlight to Electricity

A solar panel is made of photovoltaic cells — usually silicon — that produce direct current (DC) electricity when sunlight hits them. The brighter the sunlight, the more electricity the cells produce. This happens silently, with no moving parts, which is why panels are so reliable: there is very little to wear out.

Panels are mounted on your roof in an array. Each panel contributes to the total output, and the panels are wired together so their combined power flows to the next part of the system. The angle and direction of the panels matter: in Lahore, panels are generally positioned to face the sun for as much of the day as possible, with shading avoided wherever it can be.

The Inverter: The Heart of the System

Your home runs on alternating current (AC), but panels produce direct current (DC). The inverter converts DC into AC so your lights, fans, fridge and other appliances can use it. The inverter also manages the flow of power — deciding, in a hybrid system, when to send energy to your home, when to charge the batteries, and when to draw from or export to the grid.

Choosing the right inverter matters as much as choosing the panels. It must be sized to your panel array and your load, and matched to your system type — on-grid, hybrid or off-grid. Our solar inverter buying guide explains the options in more detail.

How Your Home Uses Solar Power

During the day, the electricity your panels generate flows first to whatever is running in your home — lights, fans, air conditioning, the water motor, kitchen appliances. If your panels are producing more than you are using, the surplus goes to charge your batteries (in a hybrid or off-grid system) or is exported to the grid (where net metering applies).

This is why daytime usage matters so much. A home that uses most of its power during the day gets the most direct benefit from its panels. A home whose heavy usage is in the evening benefits more from battery storage, which saves daytime energy for later.

Batteries and the Grid

In an on-grid system there are no batteries: the grid is effectively your backup, and the system stops producing during a grid outage. In a hybrid system, batteries store energy for evenings and outages. In an off-grid system, batteries are essential — they are your only source of power after sunset.

Net metering, where available, lets a grid-connected system export surplus energy and receive credit for it under your electricity provider's rules. The process and the rules depend on your provider, and we explain them during a consultation rather than promising anything in advance.

What Affects Performance

Several things affect how much electricity a solar system produces: the amount of sunlight, the angle and direction of the panels, shading from nearby buildings or trees, dust on the panels, and temperature — panels actually perform slightly less efficiently in extreme heat, which is worth knowing in Lahore's summers. Regular solar panel maintenance, especially cleaning, helps keep output where it should be.

The honest summary: solar is a straightforward technology. Sunlight hits panels, the inverter converts the power, and your home uses it. The skill is in planning the system properly for your property — which is where a good installer earns their keep. If you would like to talk through a system for your home, contact us for a free consultation.

What Happens on a Cloudy Day

Solar panels still generate electricity on cloudy days — just less of it. Diffuse sunlight still reaches the cells, so a system keeps working through overcast weather and the monsoon season, at reduced output. This is one reason installers in Lahore plan systems with some margin: a system sized only for perfect sunny days would disappoint for part of the year. Batteries and the grid cover the gaps, which is exactly what hybrid systems are designed to do.

Winter is the other seasonal change worth knowing about. Days are shorter, so total daily generation falls, even though cooler temperatures actually help panels run more efficiently. A well-planned system accounts for both seasons rather than assuming summer conditions all year round.
